One interstellar comet, two space telescopes

3I/ATLAS, only the third known visitor from another star system, seen in visible light by NEOSSat · Canadian Space Agency and in the infrared by SPHEREx · NASA.

Watch it move: the stars stay still, the comet crosses them

Each video is one sitting of one telescope, put on a fixed patch of sky. The dots that stay put are stars; the fuzzy blob that slides along the orange line is the interstellar comet. Left: Canada's NEOSSat, 58 minutes on 27 Nov 2025. Right: NASA's SPHEREx, 13 hours on 14 Dec 2025 (a much larger patch of sky, so the comet travels further).

1 · The comet's journey, both telescopes on one timeline

Every frame is centred on the object's predicted position (cyan circle) and shows the same 3′ × 3′ patch of sky, north up, east left. NEOSSat pixels are 3.0″; SPHEREx pixels are 6.15″, so its frames look blockier. Watch the comet brighten and grow a coma as it swings past the Sun (perihelion 29 Oct 2025), then fade.

Each frame is scaled by its own noise (asinh, −2 to +10σ), because the telescopes' units differ. magenta = no data.

3 · Light curves: brightness over time

Brightness in a 20″-radius aperture. NEOSSat magnitudes are calibrated on hundreds of Gaia stars in each image, then converted to millijansky. SPHEREx fluxes come from NASA's calibrated images. Two independent telescopes, two calibrations: SPHEREx at 0.7–1.0 µm reads 1.2–1.4× NEOSSat's visible flux, just what sunlight reflected by dust should give (the Sun itself is ~1.3× brighter at 0.85 µm than at 0.6 µm, per unit frequency). The orange points show carbon dioxide: SPHEREx's 4.15–4.45 µm channel, where CO₂ gas glows, outshines its neighbours.
